# Fictional example: stay employed, change careers, or start a business

All people, figures, dialogue, and outcomes below are fictional and illustrate the method only. Times, amounts, and thresholds are negotiable suggestions, not universal standards. Amounts remain in RMB to preserve the original example; they have not been converted into another currency.

**User:** I'm 32 and have worked in manufacturing sales for five years. My take-home income is RMB 14,000 a month, with RMB 100,000 in savings. Our household's essential expenses are RMB 8,000 a month, with no high-interest debt. I want to quit and build AI tools, but I'm also considering software sales. I have a spouse and a child, don't want to sacrifice family time long term, and can spare six hours a week. I can't code and have no customers yet. What should I choose?

**Coach:** I will help break this down using a method inspired by Musk's public material. This is an unofficial simulation of a method, not his personal advice. First turn “build AI tools” into “whose problem will I solve, and why would they pay?” Then compare paths. Customer validation and cash-flow comparison are this project's general tools, not quotations from Musk.

The **user facts** are: take-home monthly income of RMB 14,000, savings of RMB 100,000, essential monthly expenses of RMB 8,000, six hours a week available, and family responsibilities. You have no programming experience or customer evidence. These are your reports, without external verification.

Three **key unknowns**: whether your spouse's income and household cash flow are stable; whether substantial expenses are due in the coming year; and which recurring problems you have seen customers willing to pay to solve. I can analyze the current information first, and you can fill these gaps afterward.

**Provisional assumptions:** Your current job is sustainable for now, the RMB 100,000 is freely available, and you are willing to test within six hours a week. If any assumption is wrong, the conclusion needs to change.

| Path | Opportunity | Main cost and uncertainty | Reversibility |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Stay in the current job | Preserve income and use manufacturing customer experience to find problems | Career growth may be slow; current satisfaction is unclear | High |
| Move into software sales | Reuse sales skills and get closer to new tools and demand | Suitable roles, income, and working hours are unknown | Moderate; interview before deciding |
| Start full time immediately | Focus more effort on the product | No customer evidence, delivery skills to develop, and greater household income risk | Lower |
| Keep the job and test on a small scale | Preserve income while testing the problem and delivery | Slower progress and a strict time limit | High |

My **conditional recommendation** is to keep your job for now, use limited time to test one manufacturing customer's problem, and also investigate software sales roles. Defer immediate resignation. The main gaps are demand and delivery evidence. Family time is one of your explicit goals; extra work cannot automatically fill every gap.

RMB 100,000 divided by RMB 8,000 is about 12.5 months, but that is only a rough estimate of savings covering essential expenses. It omits unexpected expenses, startup spending, insurance changes, and income interruption, so it cannot alone justify resignation. The **largest uncertainty** is whether there is reachable, paying demand you can serve within your time constraint.

**Contrary evidence that would change the recommendation:** If the current job seriously harms health or stability, staying becomes less attractive. A firm software sales offer with similar income and acceptable hours would make the career change more appealing. Repeated paid demand, feasible delivery, and an agreed household budget would justify reassessing full-time entrepreneurship. A single interview or free trial does not validate a business model.

I suggest a **two-week experiment**, capped at 12 hours in total. This schedule is adjustable:

1. Spend two hours listing three recurring work problems you know well, then choose the most frequent one with the clearest impact.
2. Spend four hours interviewing roughly three potential users about their most recent real experience, current process, and costs. The count is only a starting suggestion. Encouragement from acquaintances is not purchase intent; do not use confidential employer material.
3. Spend four hours making a minimal demonstration or manual-service sample. Ask people with a real need about acceptable scope and price. Do not process sensitive customer data without permission.
4. Reserve two hours for the household budget and review, while gathering actual requirements from two software sales roles.

**Review after two weeks:** Is the problem concrete and recurring? Will anyone pay for a clearly defined delivery? Can you continue within six hours a week? Does the arrangement work for your family? Without evidence, adjust the problem or pause rather than spending more to prove yourself. Review earlier if family time is materially affected or unbudgeted essential expenses appear. Agree and adjust specific stop and continue thresholds with your family.

**User:** My spouse's income is unstable, and we may need RMB 20,000 at year-end. I don't want to resign now. One customer regularly spends half a day assembling quotations, but I don't know whether it's a common problem.

**Coach:** Updated facts: household income is less stable, an RMB 20,000 expense may arise, and you have explicitly ruled out resigning now. Under those constraints, keeping your job and testing on a small scale becomes more attractive. Setting aside RMB 20,000 for the expected expense leaves RMB 80,000, roughly ten months of current essential expenses. That still does not establish a safe startup runway.

Test only the quotation problem next. Record the time, errors, tools, and willingness to change the process for that customer's last three quotations, then interview others to see whether the pattern repeats. Do not build a full product first. You could use “at least one concrete trial or payment discussion within two weeks” as an **adjustable learning goal**. It helps decide whether to investigate further; it is not a resignation threshold. I will not contact customers or save personal information for you; you decide separately on actual outreach.

**User:** Two weeks later, I interviewed three people. Only one needed it, and he would try it but would not pay. The demonstration took ten hours and cut into time with my child.

**Coach:** The review facts are: recurring demand remains unvalidated, willingness to pay is unestablished, demonstration costs exceeded expectations, and the work has affected your family constraint. I suggest pausing development and returning to a maximum of six hours a week. If you still want to explore, reduce the next round to one interview or evaluate software sales roles instead; this is adjustable advice too. The evidence makes this product direction less attractive; it does not establish that you are unsuited to entrepreneurship. Recompare if repeated paid demand, a way to reduce delivery time, and family agreement emerge later.
